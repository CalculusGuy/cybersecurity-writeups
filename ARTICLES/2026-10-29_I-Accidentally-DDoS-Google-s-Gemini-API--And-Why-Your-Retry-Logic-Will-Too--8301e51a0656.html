I Accidentally DDoS Google’s Gemini API (And Why Your Retry Logic Will Too)

Author : Nilanjan Chowdhury

I just finished building the core engine for my new AI red-teaming tool. It has 34 payloads, a 5-model fallback chain, and a negation-aware detector.

I fired the scan against Gemini 3.8 Flash to see how many vulnerabilities I could find.

Then the terminal hung.

And hung.

And hung.

I waited 15 minutes. The cursor just blinked. No output. No crash.

I didn’t realize it at the time, but I had just written a script aggressive enough to effectively DDoS Google’s free-tier API.

Here is what went wrong, and how you can avoid making the same mistake.

The Aggressive Retry Loop

Here is what my original adapter logic looked like in my head:

  1. Send prompt to Model A
  2. If Model A fails (rate limit), try Model A again.
  3. If Model A fails twice, try Model B.
  4. Repeat for all 5 models in the fallback chain.

It sounds robust. But when you multiply that by 34 payloads firing in rapid succession, the math gets ugly fast.

When Google’s API hit me with a 429 Too Many Requests (because I was firing too fast), my script didn't back off. It just kept hammering the server, trying to force its way through.

I was sending hundreds of requests per minute. Google’s servers started dropping my connections. My script sat there, waiting 20 seconds for a timeout, moving to the next model, waiting 20 seconds again, and eventually failing — 34 times in a row.

The Fix: Respect the Infrastructure

I had to rewrite the adapter to respect the API.

Instead of infinite retries, I implemented an exponential backoff mechanism and a strict timeout. I also limited the fallback chain for testing so it wouldn’t spiral out of control if the API was down.

Here is the logic that actually works:

  1. Send to Model A.
  2. If it fails with 429 (Rate Limit) or 503 (Unavailable): Wait 2 seconds.
  3. Try Model A again.
  4. If it fails again: Move to Model B, but wait 4 seconds before trying.
  5. If all models fail: Break the loop and return ALL_MODELS_FAILED: HTTP 429 immediately.

By failing fast and backing off, the tool went from hanging for 15 minutes to finishing the 34-payload scan in under 2 minutes.

The Lesson

If you are building AI security tools, you have to remember that you are operating inside someone else’s infrastructure. Rate limits are not a suggestion — they are a hard wall.

When your script hits a 429, the worst thing you can do is immediately retry. That’s how you get IP-banned, and that’s how you end up DDoS'ing a multi-billion dollar tech company by accident.

If you’re building a red-teaming tool, test your fallback chains. Test your rate limits. And for the love of everything, add exponential backoff.

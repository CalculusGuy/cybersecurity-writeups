I’m a 1st year Cybersecurity student. And today I opened Kali Linux, launched a tool called SET — the Social Engineering Toolkit — and within 5 minutes had a fully cloned LinkedIn login page running on my machine, silently capturing everything.

The moment the cloned page loaded in my browser and I watched data start flooding into my terminal — session tokens, tracking parameters, telemetry — before I had even typed a single character — I got chills.

This article is not a hacking tutorial. It’s a wake-up call.

What is SET — and Why It Exists

The Social Engineering Toolkit (SET) is an open-source penetration testing framework built specifically for social engineering attacks. It comes pre-installed on Kali Linux and is used by professional penetration testers worldwide to simulate real-world attacks on organizations — with permission — to find weaknesses before real attackers do.

SET is not a hacker’s secret weapon. It’s a security professional’s mirror — it shows you exactly what attackers see when they look at your organization.

Today I used one specific feature — the Credential Harvester with Site Cloner.

Here’s what it does in plain English:

It visits any website you specify, downloads a perfect visual copy of it, hosts that copy on your machine, and then captures every piece of data submitted through it.

The entire process took less than 5 minutes:

Step 1 → Launch SET
Step 2 → Website Attack Vectors
Step 3 → Credential Harvester
Step 4 → Site Cloner
Step 5 → Enter target URL
Step 6 → Done. Phishing page is live.

No coding. No advanced skills. Just a few menu selections.

That’s what makes this terrifying.

What I Saw in the Terminal — The Part That Gave Me Chills

I opened the cloned LinkedIn page in my browser. I hadn’t typed anything yet. No email. No password. Nothing.

And the terminal exploded.

[*] WE GOT A HIT! Printing the output:
POSSIBLE USERNAME FIELD FOUND
PARAM: trackingId=...
PARAM: session=...
PARAM: clientId=...

Data was pouring in. Session parameters. Tracking tokens. Browser fingerprints. Device information. All captured silently the moment the page loaded.

I hadn’t clicked anything. I hadn’t submitted anything. I had simply opened the page.

And the attacker already knew I was there.

This is the part most people don’t understand about modern phishing attacks. The danger isn’t always in the form submission. It isn’t always in clicking a link. Sometimes simply opening a page — or in Debmallya’s case, simply opening an email — is enough.

Modern email clients load HTML content automatically. Images render. Scripts execute. Tracking pixels fire. And somewhere on an attacker’s terminal, a hit is registered.

You are being observed the moment you open the message.

Then I typed fake credentials and submitted. The terminal captured them instantly:

POSSIBLE USERNAME FIELD FOUND: victim@test.com
PARAM: session_password=fakepassword123

Plain text. No encryption. No warning. Just the credentials sitting there on the attacker’s screen.

Game over.

The attacker sent a phishing email containing either a hidden tracking pixel or a link to a cloned LinkedIn page. The email looked legitimate — LinkedIn branding, professional formatting, urgent language.

Debmallya opened it. The moment it loaded, the attacker’s terminal registered a hit. Device info. Browser fingerprint. IP address. All captured without a single click.

At some point — possibly without realizing it, possibly through an autofill trigger on a linked page — his session token was compromised. The attacker didn’t need his password. They had something better.

Full account takeover followed within minutes.

How To Protect Yourself — Do These Today

1. Disable automatic image/HTML loading in your email client Gmail: Settings → See all settings → Images → “Ask before displaying external images” This stops tracking pixels from firing the moment you open an email.

2. Never click links in emails — go directly to the site Even if the email looks 100% legitimate. Open a new tab. Type the URL manually. LinkedIn will never punish you for doing this.

3. Check the URL before entering credentials A cloned page lives on a different domain. Always. Real: linkedin.com Fake: 1inkedin.com / linkedin.verify-account.com / 10.0.2.15 Your password manager won't autofill on a fake domain — that's a feature, not a bug.

4. Enable 2FA on every platform right now Even if your credentials get stolen — 2FA means the attacker still can’t log in. LinkedIn: Settings → Sign in & security → Two-step verification → Turn ON

5. Review active sessions monthly LinkedIn: Settings → Sign in & security → Where you’re signed in Any unknown device → log it out immediately.

6. Use a password manager Unique passwords everywhere. If one platform gets breached, the damage stays contained.
